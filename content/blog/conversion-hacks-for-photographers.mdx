## Understanding Focal Length and Crop Factors

One of the most common conversions photographers face is determining the equivalent focal length of a lens on a camera with a sensor smaller than the standard 35mm "full-frame". This is done using a crop factor.

### The Crop Factor Math

*   **Full-Frame Sensor:** 35mm equivalent (Crop factor of 1.0x)
*   **APS-C (Sony, Nikon, Fuji):** Crop factor of 1.5x
*   **APS-C (Canon):** Crop factor of 1.6x
*   **Micro Four Thirds (MFT):** Crop factor of 2.0x

**Mental Math Hack:**
If you are shooting with a 50mm lens on a Micro Four Thirds camera, simply double the focal length (50mm * 2 = 100mm equivalent). On a Sony APS-C, multiply by 1.5. A quick way to multiply by 1.5 in your head is to take half of the number and add it to the original (e.g., for a 50mm lens: half is 25, so 50 + 25 = 75mm).

## Print Sizes: Millimetres, Centimetres, and Inches

When preparing your work for print or exhibition, you'll frequently need to convert between metric and imperial units. Print labs in the US and UK often use inches, while European labs use centimetres or millimetres.

### The Essential Conversion Factor

The most important number to memorize is **25.4**.
*   **1 inch = 25.4 millimetres (mm)**
*   **1 inch = 2.54 centimetres (cm)**

**Quick Approximations:**
*   An 8x10 inch print is roughly 20x25 cm.
*   A 16x20 inch print is roughly 40x50 cm.
*   A 24x36 inch print is roughly 60x90 cm.

While these approximations are helpful for visualizing scale, you need absolute precision when ordering custom frames or cutting mats. This is where a digital tool like UnitFlow becomes indispensable.

## Lighting and Distance: Inverse Square Law

In studio photography, understanding the relationship between distance and light intensity is crucial. The Inverse Square Law states that the intensity of light is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the source.

If you double the distance between your subject and your light source, you don't get half the light; you get one-quarter of the light.

When working in different studios, you might encounter measurements in feet, yards, or meters.

*   **1 meter is approximately 3.28 feet.**
*   **1 yard is 3 feet (or exactly 0.9144 meters).**

**Mental Math Hack:**
If you need to quickly estimate meters to feet, multiply by 3 and add 10%. (e.g., 5 meters * 3 = 15 + 1.5 = 16.5 feet). For exact measurements when setting up complex lighting grids, always rely on a precise calculator.

## Color Temperature: Kelvin

Color temperature is measured in Kelvin (K). While you don't typically "convert" Kelvin to another unit like Fahrenheit or Celsius in everyday photography, understanding the scale is vital for setting custom white balance.

*   **Candlelight:** ~1500K (Very warm/orange)
*   **Incandescent Bulb (Tungsten):** ~3200K (Warm)
*   **Daylight (Midday Sun):** ~5500K (Neutral/Blueish)
*   **Overcast Sky/Shade:** ~6500K - 8000K (Cool/Blue)

## Sensor Sizes: The Megapixel Myth and Pixel Pitch

Photographers often get caught up in the "Megapixel Myth," assuming more pixels always equals better image quality. However, the physical size of the sensor and the size of individual pixels (pixel pitch, measured in microns or micrometers, µm) play a massive role in low-light performance and dynamic range.

A full-frame 24-megapixel sensor will have much larger individual pixels than an APS-C 24-megapixel sensor. Larger pixels gather more light, leading to cleaner images at high ISOs.

*   **1 micron (µm) = 0.001 millimetres (mm)**

When reading technical specifications for new camera bodies, understanding these micro-measurements can help you make informed purchasing decisions based on the actual physics of the sensor, rather than marketing hype.
