When working with 3D printing, the most common challenge is scaling models correctly when importing STLs or OBJs that were designed in a different unit system. By standardizing on millimeters and using reliable tools for complex calculations, you can eliminate scale-related print failures.

## The Supremacy of the Millimeter

In almost all 3D printing workflows—from hobbyist FDM printers to industrial SLA machines—the millimeter (mm) is the lingua franca. Slicing software like Cura, PrusaSlicer, and Chitubox defaults to millimeters. If you import a model that was designed in inches without scaling it, it will appear tiny (25.4 times smaller) on the build plate.

### 1. Inches to Millimeters (The Golden Rule)
The most critical conversion factor every 3D printing enthusiast must memorize is:
**1 inch = 25.4 mm**

If you have a model designed in inches and your slicer imports it as millimeters, you simply need to scale the model uniformly by 2540% (since scaling factors are often percentages) or apply a 25.4 scale multiplier.

### 2. Centimeters to Millimeters
While less common than the inch-to-mm mix-up, some designers work in centimeters. The conversion is straightforward:
**1 cm = 10 mm**
If your 10 cm object imports as 10 mm, scale it by 1000% (or a factor of 10).

## Advanced Conversions: Filament Volume and Weight

Beyond simple dimensions, 3D printing often requires converting between volume and weight to calculate material costs and ensure you have enough filament to complete a job. Slicers typically provide estimates in meters (length) and grams (weight), but understanding how these relate to volume is crucial.

### Calculating Filament Weight from Volume
To convert the volume of your printed object into weight, you need to know the density of the filament you are using. Common densities include:
- **PLA:** ~1.24 g/cm³
- **ABS:** ~1.04 g/cm³
- **PETG:** ~1.27 g/cm³

If your slicer indicates a model volume of 50 cm³, and you are using PLA, the conversion is:
**50 cm³ × 1.24 g/cm³ = 62 grams**

This calculation allows you to accurately predict if that remaining spool of filament is sufficient for your overnight print.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid in 3D Printing Conversions

Even experienced makers can fall victim to conversion errors. Here are the most common pitfalls:

1. **Non-Uniform Scaling:** Always ensure the uniform scaling lock is engaged in your slicer when converting units. Scaling only the X, Y, or Z axis independently will distort your model.
2. **Confusing Scale Factors and Percentages:** Remember that a scale factor of 25.4 is equal to a scaling percentage of 2540%. Entering 25.4% by mistake will result in a microscopic print.
3. **Ignoring Tolerances:** When converting dimensions for functional parts (like interlocking gears or snap-fit joints), remember that conversion rounding can affect tight tolerances. Always leave a 0.1 mm to 0.2 mm clearance.
