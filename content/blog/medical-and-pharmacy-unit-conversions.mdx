This guide is designed to help healthcare professionals, pharmacists, and students master the most critical unit conversions used in daily practice.

When working with medical dosages, mental math is often necessary, but it should always be verified. The metric system is universally used in healthcare, meaning most conversions involve moving the decimal point by factors of 10, 100, or 1,000.

## The Most Critical Medical Conversion: Milligrams and Micrograms

One of the most common—and potentially dangerous—conversions in pharmacy is between milligrams (mg) and micrograms (mcg).

- **1 milligram (mg) = 1,000 micrograms (mcg)**
- **1 microgram (mcg) = 0.001 milligrams (mg)**

When prescribing or administering high-alert medications like Levothyroxine or Fentanyl, confusing these two units can lead to a 1,000-fold dosing error. Always write out "micrograms" or use the standard abbreviation "mcg" (avoiding "µg" in handwritten notes, which can be misread as "mg").

## Common Weight and Volume Conversions in Healthcare

### Weight Conversions for Patient Dosing
Many medications are dosed based on patient weight, often requiring a conversion from pounds (lbs) to kilograms (kg).
- **1 Kilogram (kg) = 2.2 Pounds (lbs)**
- *Mental Math Hack:* To quickly convert pounds to kilograms, divide by 2, then subtract 10% of that result. (e.g., 200 lbs / 2 = 100; 100 - 10 = 90 kg).

### Liquid Volume Conversions
Oral medications are frequently dispensed in milliliters (mL), but patients often use household measurements like teaspoons or tablespoons.
- **1 Teaspoon (tsp) = 5 Milliliters (mL)**
- **1 Tablespoon (tbsp) = 15 Milliliters (mL)**
- **1 Fluid Ounce (fl oz) = 30 Milliliters (mL)**

*Note:* It is always recommended to use oral syringes rather than household spoons to ensure accurate dosing.

## Preventing Errors: Best Practices for Medical Calculations

1. **The Leading Zero Rule:** Always use a leading zero before a decimal point for doses less than one (e.g., write `0.5 mg`, never `.5 mg`).
2. **The Trailing Zero Rule:** Never use a trailing zero after a decimal point (e.g., write `5 mg`, never `5.0 mg`).
3. **Use a Deterministic Tool:** For high-stakes calculations or complex IV drip rates, rely on a dedicated tool rather than a generic calculator. Use our [precision unit converter](/) to ensure zero-error calculations.
